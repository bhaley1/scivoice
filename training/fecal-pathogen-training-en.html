Fecal Pathogen Isolation Training
Clinical isolation of enteric bacterial pathogens from human stool, following CDC and WHO laboratory methods
1 About This Course

This course teaches the laboratory isolation of enteric bacterial pathogens from human fecal specimens — the bench work of recovering, growing, and identifying a viable organism. It does not cover clinical diagnosis or patient management.

Culture-first principle

Culture is the reference method. Molecular tests can detect a pathogen's nucleic acid but yield no organism — and a live isolate is required for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, serotyping, and outbreak characterization. Where molecular methods are used, the specimen must be reflexed to culture.

By the end of this course, participants will be able to:

  • Isolate and identify the major enteric bacterial pathogens from human stool specimens
  • Apply CDC and WHO standard isolation protocols, using the correct selective and enrichment media
  • Perform biochemical and serological confirmatory tests
  • Maintain biosafety and quality-control practices
  • Understand the supporting role of molecular methods and the reflex-culture principle

Specimen Collection & Transport
Collecting and transporting human fecal specimens for bacterial isolation
1 Collecting the Specimen
Method source: CDC
  • Preferred specimen: freshly passed stool, collected as early as possible after onset and before antimicrobial treatment
  • Container: clean, leak-proof, wide-mouth screw-cap container
  • Volume: 1-2 g of faecal material, or 2-3 mL if liquid
  • Prioritise portions containing mucus, blood, or pus
  • Rectal swabs are acceptable only when a stool sample cannot be produced — visible faecal material must be present on the swab
2 Transport — Cary-Blair Medium
Method source: CDC
  • Process promptly: ideally within 2 hours of collection
  • If delayed: place the specimen in Cary-Blair transport medium
  • Cary-Blair is a semi-solid medium suitable for most enteric bacteria, holding viability up to several days
  • Storage: may be held at 2-8°C; cholera-suspect specimens in Cary-Blair can ship at room temperature

Why transport matters

Enteric pathogens lose viability with delay, faecal pH shift, and transport stress. Shigella in particular is fragile. Correct transport protects the organism so it can still be cultured.

3 Biosafety
Method source: CDC

Personal protective equipment:

  • Gloves: changed between specimens
  • Laboratory coat: fastened, long-sleeved
  • Eye protection: where splashes are possible
  • Closed footwear

Biosafety Level 2

Handle all faecal specimens at BSL-2. Treat every specimen as potentially infectious.

Specimen Collection Checklist

  • Fresh stool collected before antimicrobial treatment
  • Leak-proof container, correctly labelled
  • Processed within 2 h, or placed in Cary-Blair
  • BSL-2 PPE worn throughout
Salmonella Isolation
Isolating non-typhoidal Salmonella from human stool, following CDC laboratory methods
1 The Isolation Workflow
Method source: CDC

Recovering Salmonella from stool follows a four-stage pattern. Stool contains a heavy background of normal flora, so the early stages exist to suppress that flora and let the pathogen grow before the identification stages begin.

2 Selective Enrichment
Method source: CDC
  • Inoculate an enrichment broth — Selenite (SEL) or Tetrathionate (TT) — from the stool specimen
  • Incubate per the medium manufacturer's instructions before subculturing to selective agar
  • Why it matters: enrichment raises sensitivity by letting small numbers of Salmonella multiply while faecal flora is held back

Choosing the broth

If S. Typhi is a consideration, Selenite is generally preferred. Always review the manufacturer's instructions for incubation time and temperature — these vary by product.

3 Isolation on Selective Agar
Method source: CDC

Subculture the enrichment broth onto at least two selective media. XLD and HE are the most widely used; CHROMagar, SS, or XLT-4 are alternatives.

Reading the plate — decision point

Classic Salmonella on XLD: red or pink colony, often with a distinct black centre. The black centre is H₂S — a useful pointer, but not proof on its own.

Decide: pick 2-3 well-separated colonies of each suspect morphology for confirmation. When colonies are crowded or overlapping, sub-culture to a fresh plate first to get pure, single colonies — never run confirmatory tests off a mixed growth.

Ambiguous results — what to watch

H₂S-negative Salmonella exist — a red colony with no black centre is not automatically excluded. Other organisms produce H₂S — Proteus can give black-centred colonies and mislead. This is exactly why a second selective medium and biochemical confirmation are required: never identify Salmonella from XLD appearance alone.

4 Biochemical Confirmation
Method source: CDC

Pick suspect colonies to confirmatory media. A triple sugar iron (TSI) slant plus urea is a common screening pair; many laboratories use MALDI-TOF or a panel such as API-20E for identification.

TestTypical SalmonellaInterpretation
TSI reactionK/A (alkaline slant / acid butt)Glucose fermented, lactose not

UreaseNegativeHelps separate from Proteus

After confirmation

Once Salmonella is confirmed, the isolate is the basis for serotyping and, where available, whole-genome sequencing for characterisation. A viable isolate — not a molecular result — is what makes that possible. Method source: CDC

5 Common Failure Modes

Practical laboratory guidance

The points below are experience-based good practice, not a cited CDC/WHO protocol step. They address why an isolation attempt fails.

  • Prior antibiotics → false negative. If the patient received antimicrobials before collection, Salmonella may not grow even when infection is present. Record antibiotic history; a negative culture does not exclude infection.
  • Transport delay → loss of viability. Unpreserved stool left too long, or skipped Cary-Blair, lowers recovery. If transport was delayed, note it — it informs how a negative result is read.
  • Skipped enrichment → missed light infections. Plating stool directly onto selective agar without enrichment can miss low numbers of organisms. Enrichment is what catches them.
  • Reading a mixed plate. Confirmatory tests run on a colony that is not pure give uninterpretable results. Always sub-culture to a single, well-separated colony first.
  • Expired or mis-stored media. Selective agars lose selectivity past expiry or if stored badly. Run media QC with control strains before trusting a plate.

XLD agar — what is this?
Suspect Salmonella. Red colonies with black centres (H₂S+). Pick well-separated colonies for confirmation — appearance alone is not proof.
XLD agar — what is this?
Not Salmonella. Yellow colonies = lactose/xylose fermentation with no H₂S. Typical of coliform background flora — do not pick for confirmation.
XLD agar — what is this?
Ambiguous — swarming growth. A spreading film over the plate suggests Proteus, which can also blacken. Sub-culture an isolated colony; confirm with urease (Salmonella is urease-negative).

Salmonella Isolation Checklist

  • Enrichment broth (Selenite or TT) inoculated
  • Subcultured to at least two selective media
  • Suspect colonies picked pure for confirmation
  • TSI and urease (or MALDI-TOF / panel) performed
  • Confirmed isolate retained for serotyping / WGS
Shigella Isolation
Isolating Shigella from human stool, following CDC laboratory methods
1 A Fragile Organism — Why Speed Matters
Method source: CDC

Shigella is delicate and survives poorly outside the host. Unlike Salmonella, it is plated directly onto selective agar — enrichment is of limited value and recovery falls quickly with delay.

Time sensitivity

Process within 2 hours. If any delay is expected, the specimen must travel in Cary-Blair — acidification of stool kills Shigella quickly.

2 Isolation on Selective Agar
Method source: CDC

Plate directly onto selective media — XLD, HE, and SS are standard. Shigella does not ferment lactose and does not produce H₂S.

MediumShigella appearanceKey distinction
XLDRed/pink, no black centreH₂S-negative — separates from Salmonella
HEGreen, moistNon-lactose-fermenting
SSColourlessNon-lactose, H₂S-negative

Reading the plate — decision point

The key signal is the absence of black centres on XLD — a red colony with no H₂S points toward Shigella rather than Salmonella. Pick non-lactose-fermenting, H₂S-negative colonies, pure, for confirmation.

Ambiguous results

Shigella and E. coli are closely related and can look alike on selective agar. Colony appearance is only a screen — biochemical and serological confirmation is always required to separate them.

3 Confirmation
Method source: CDC
TestTypical ShigellaWhy it helps
TSIK/A, no gas, H₂S-negativeGlucose only; no H₂S
MotilityNon-motileKey separation from E. coli
UreaseNegativeSeparates from Proteus

Serological confirmation with group-specific antisera, and species/biochemical identification (MALDI-TOF or a panel), complete the identification.

4 Common Failure Modes

Practical laboratory guidance

Experience-based good practice, not a cited CDC/WHO protocol step.

  • Transport delay → dead organism. Shigella is the most fragile pathogen in this course. A skipped or delayed Cary-Blair step is the most common reason for a false-negative culture.
  • Relying on enrichment. Treating Shigella like Salmonella and enriching instead of plating directly loses organisms — direct plating of a fresh specimen is correct.
  • Confusing it with E. coli. Stopping at colony appearance risks calling E. coli a Shigella or vice versa. Always confirm with motility and serology.
  • Prior antibiotics. As with all enteric pathogens, prior antimicrobials reduce recovery — record the history.

Suspect Shigella. Red/pink colonies with no black centres. Pick pure colonies; confirm with motility (non-motile) and serology.
XLD agar — what is this?
Not Shigella. Black centres mean H₂S production — this points to Salmonella. Shigella is H₂S-negative.
XLD agar — what is this?
Not Shigella. A yellow plate means lactose/xylose fermenters have acidified the agar — coliform background flora. Look for non-fermenting colonies instead.

Shigella Isolation Checklist

  • Fresh specimen plated directly within 2 h (or Cary-Blair used)
  • Plated to XLD, HE, and SS
  • Non-lactose, H₂S-negative colonies picked pure
  • Motility (non-motile) confirmed
  • Serological confirmation performed
STEC / EHEC Isolation
Shiga toxin-producing E. coli from human stool, following CDC laboratory methods
1 Two Things to Find
Method source: CDC

STEC isolation is unusual: CDC recommends doing two things on every stool submitted for culture — culture-based screening for E. coli O157, and testing for Shiga toxin or the genes stx1 / stx2. O157 is the most common cause of severe disease, but non-O157 STEC also cause illness and are missed by O157 culture alone.

2 Isolation on SMAC Agar
Method source: CDC

Sorbitol-MacConkey (SMAC) agar exploits a quirk of E. coli O157: unlike most E. coli, it does not ferment sorbitol.

OrganismSMAC appearanceSorbitol
E. coli O157ColourlessNegative
Ordinary E. coliPinkPositive

Reading the plate — decision point

Pick colourless (sorbitol-negative) colonies as suspect O157. Confirm with O157 antiserum agglutination. But remember the second test still matters — see the failure modes.

Ambiguous results

SMAC only catches O157. Non-O157 STEC ferment sorbitol and look pink — indistinguishable from harmless E. coli on this plate. A clean SMAC does not rule out STEC; the Shiga toxin / stx test is what catches them.

3 Shiga Toxin Detection
Method source: CDC

CDC recommends testing for Shiga toxin or its genes on all stools submitted for culture. The genes stx1 and stx2 define STEC; eae (intimin) is a related virulence marker.

Clinical urgency

STEC can lead to haemolytic uraemic syndrome, particularly in children. A positive result needs prompt communication and any isolate retained for public health investigation.

Detection vs isolate

A Shiga toxin or stx-positive result identifies STEC but yields no organism. Where a molecular method is positive, the specimen should be cultured to recover a viable isolate for characterisation. Method source: CDC

4 Common Failure Modes

Practical laboratory guidance

Experience-based good practice, not a cited CDC/WHO protocol step.

  • O157-only testing → missed STEC. The single most important STEC error: culturing SMAC for O157 but skipping the Shiga toxin / stx test. Non-O157 STEC look like normal E. coli and will be missed entirely.
  • Discarding the isolate after a positive toxin test. A toxin-positive result is not the end — the isolate is needed for serotyping and outbreak work. Culture and retain it.
  • Not flagging urgency. STEC carries HUS risk; a result left in a routine queue delays a response that may matter clinically.
5 Practice — Read the Plate

Tap each plate to reveal what it shows.

SMAC agar — what is this?
Suspect E. coli O157. Colourless = sorbitol-negative. Confirm with O157 antiserum agglutination.
SMAC agar — what is this?
Pink — sorbitol-positive. Not O157. But beware: a non-O157 STEC would look exactly like this. A clean SMAC does not rule out STEC — the stx test still applies.
SMAC agar — what is this?
Mixed growth. Both colourless and pink colonies present. Pick and confirm the colourless one for O157 — and still run the Shiga toxin / stx test regardless.

STEC / EHEC Isolation Checklist

  • Stool plated on SMAC for O157 screening
  • Shiga toxin / stx test performed on the same specimen
  • Colourless colonies confirmed with O157 antiserum
  • Toxin-positive specimens cultured to recover an isolate
  • Positive result communicated promptly; isolate retained
Campylobacter Isolation
Isolating Campylobacter from human stool, following CDC laboratory methods
1 A Fastidious Organism
Method source: CDC

Campylobacter is fastidious and needs conditions most enteric bacteria do not: a reduced-oxygen (microaerophilic) atmosphere, a higher incubation temperature, and longer incubation. CDC describes isolation from stool or rectal swabs on selective media incubated under reduced oxygen at 42°C for 72 hours.

Microaerophilic atmosphere

Campylobacter needs reduced oxygen — roughly 5% O₂, 10% CO₂. This is created with a commercial gas-generating sachet in a sealed jar, or an equivalent system.

2 The Atmosphere — Equipment
Method source: CDC

Decision point — getting the atmosphere right

If the jar is not properly sealed, or the sachet is expired or omitted, the atmosphere is wrong and Campylobacter will not grow — producing a false negative with no visible warning. Confirm the jar seals fully and use a fresh sachet each run.

3 Isolation & Confirmation
Method source: CDC

Plate onto a Campylobacter-selective medium — Karmali or Skirrow are standard. Incubate microaerophilically at 42°C; growth may take up to 72 hours.

TestC. jejuniNotes
OxidasePositiveRapid screening test
CatalasePositive—
MicroscopyCurved/spiral Gram-negative rodsCharacteristic darting motility

Reading the plate — decision point

Campylobacter colonies are flat, moist, and often spreading — easy to overlook if you expect raised, discrete colonies. Confirm suspect growth with oxidase and microscopy: the curved/spiral Gram-negative rod is highly characteristic.

4 Common Failure Modes

Practical laboratory guidance

Experience-based good practice, not a cited CDC/WHO protocol step.

  • Wrong atmosphere → no growth. The most common Campylobacter failure: an unsealed jar or expired sachet. The plate looks blank and the result is falsely negative. Verify the seal and sachet every run.
  • Incubating at 35-37°C. Routine enteric temperature is too low for selective C. jejuni isolation — 42°C is required.
  • Reading too early. Discarding a plate at 24-48 h misses slower growth — hold to 72 h before calling it negative.
  • Transport delay. Campylobacter is fragile and oxygen-sensitive; delayed specimens not placed in Cary-Blair lose viability.
  • Missing flat colonies. Spreading, film-like growth is easy to miss — inspect the plate carefully.
5 Practice — Read the Plate

Tap each plate to reveal what it shows.

Karmali agar, 42°C — what is this?
Suspect Campylobacter. Flat, moist, grey spreading colonies. Confirm with oxidase (positive) and microscopy (curved Gram-negative rods).
Karmali agar — no growth at 72 h. What now?
Check the atmosphere before calling it negative. A blank plate is often a failed microaerophilic jar — unsealed lid or expired sachet — not a true negative. Verify the seal and sachet, and confirm 42°C was used.
Karmali agar — what is this?
Spreading, film-like growth. Campylobacter often grows as a moist film rather than discrete colonies — easy to overlook. Sample the film, confirm with oxidase and microscopy.

Campylobacter Isolation Checklist

  • Plated on Karmali or Skirrow selective agar
  • Microaerophilic jar sealed; fresh gas sachet used
  • Incubated at 42°C, held up to 72 h
  • Oxidase and microscopy performed on suspect growth
Vibrio cholerae Isolation
Isolating Vibrio cholerae from human stool, following WHO/GTFCC and CDC laboratory methods
1 Isolation Under Outbreak Conditions
Method source: WHO / GTFCC Method source: CDC

Vibrio cholerae isolation is built around two ideas: cholera is usually investigated during outbreaks, and the organism tolerates alkaline conditions that suppress competing flora. The WHO/GTFCC cholera laboratory job aids and the CDC dysentery and cholera manual both describe this workflow for low-resource settings.

Outbreak reporting

Suspected cholera has public-health urgency. A presumptive V. cholerae O1/O139 result must be communicated promptly under national and international reporting requirements.

2 Alkaline Enrichment
Method source: WHO / GTFCC
  • Alkaline peptone water (APW) at around pH 8.4-8.6 enriches V. cholerae while suppressing other enteric flora
  • Incubate briefly — Vibrio grows rapidly and concentrates at the surface as a film
  • Subculture from the surface of the APW onto selective agar

Why alkaline

V. cholerae tolerates high pH better than competing enteric organisms. The alkaline step is a simple, low-cost way to give it a head start.

3 Isolation on TCBS & Confirmation
Method source: WHO / GTFCC Method source: CDC

Thiosulfate-citrate-bile-sucrose (TCBS) agar is the selective medium for Vibrio. V. cholerae ferments sucrose and forms distinctive yellow colonies.

TestV. choleraeNotes
TCBS colony colourYellowSucrose-positive
OxidasePositiveRapid screening test
SerologyO1 or O139 agglutinationConfirms epidemic potential

Reading the plate — decision point

Pick yellow, oxidase-positive colonies as suspect V. cholerae. Confirm with O1 and O139 antisera — only those serogroups carry epidemic potential and trigger the outbreak response.

Ambiguous results

Other Vibrio species grow on TCBS — some as green (non-sucrose) colonies, some yellow. Colony colour is a screen only; oxidase plus O1/O139 serology is what confirms epidemic V. cholerae.

4 Common Failure Modes

Practical laboratory guidance

Experience-based good practice, not a cited WHO/CDC protocol step.

  • Skipping alkaline enrichment. In light infections or convalescent specimens, plating straight to TCBS without APW can miss low numbers of organisms.
  • Subculturing from the wrong part of the APW. Vibrio concentrates as a surface film — sample the surface, not the depth of the broth.
  • Stopping at yellow colonies. A yellow TCBS colony is presumptive only. Other Vibrio mimic it; O1/O139 serology is required before reporting cholera.
  • Delayed reporting. Cholera is an outbreak pathogen — a presumptive result held in a routine queue delays the public-health response.
5 Practice — Read the Plate

Tap each plate to reveal what it shows.

TCBS agar — what is this?
Suspect V. cholerae. Bright yellow colonies = sucrose fermentation. Confirm with oxidase and O1/O139 serology — only those serogroups are epidemic.
TCBS agar — what is this?
Green colonies — not V. cholerae. Green = non-sucrose-fermenting. Other Vibrio species (e.g. V. parahaemolyticus) appear this way. Not epidemic cholera.
TCBS agar — what is this?
Mixed yellow and green growth. Pick the yellow, oxidase-positive colony and confirm with O1/O139 serology. The colour alone never confirms cholera.

Vibrio cholerae Isolation Checklist

  • Specimen enriched in alkaline peptone water
  • Surface film subcultured to TCBS agar
  • Yellow, oxidase-positive colonies picked
  • O1 / O139 serology performed
  • Presumptive result reported promptly
Molecular Methods & Characterisation
The supporting role of molecular methods in a culture-based course
1 Why Culture Comes First
Method source: CDC

Molecular tests — PCR panels, antigen tests — can detect a pathogen's nucleic acid or antigen directly in stool, often faster than culture. They are useful. But they have one decisive limitation for this course: they yield no organism.

What a culture isolate gives you that a molecular result cannot

A viable isolate is required for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, for serotyping, and for whole-genome sequencing in outbreak investigation. A molecular-positive result tells you something is present; it does not give you the organism those next steps need.

2 The Reflex-Culture Principle
Method source: CDC

CDC's guidance is consistent across enteric pathogens: when a culture-independent test is positive, the specimen should be reflexed to culture to recover an isolate.

3 STEC — Where Molecular Testing Is Recommended
Method source: CDC

STEC is the clearest case where CDC recommends a molecular target alongside culture. Testing for Shiga toxin or the genes stx1 and stx2 on all stools submitted for culture catches non-O157 STEC that O157 culture alone would miss.

4 Characterisation of the Isolate
Method source: CDC

Once an isolate is in hand, molecular methods come into their own — not for detection, but for characterisation: whole-genome sequencing for serotyping and subtyping, which underpins outbreak investigation.

The throughline of this course

Detect with molecular tools where appropriate; recover the organism by culture; characterise the isolate. Culture is the step that makes everything downstream possible.

Procurement in low-resource settings

Media may be bought ready-poured or prepared in-house from dehydrated powder. Plan for cold-chain transport where required and for lead times on international orders. Verify that any product meets local regulatory requirements.

Practical laboratory guidance

Always run media quality control with known control strains before using a new lot — expired or poorly stored selective media lose their selectivity and cause both false negatives and misidentification. Track lot numbers and expiry dates.
